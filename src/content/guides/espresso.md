intro:
  - >-
    Espresso is coffee brewed under pressure. Hot water is forced through a compacted bed
    of finely ground coffee in around half a minute, producing a small, concentrated drink
    with a layer of crema on top. It is the base for cappuccino, flat white, latte and most
    of what a café menu offers.
  - >-
    It is also the least forgiving method on this site, and it is worth knowing why. Every
    other brewer gives you minutes to work with; espresso gives you seconds. Half a gram of
    coffee, one click of the grinder or a tilted tamp all show up clearly in the cup, which
    is what makes a scale and a repeatable routine genuinely necessary rather than fussy.
  - >-
    The good news is that the process is short and the feedback is immediate. Once you fix
    the dose and the yield and change only the grind, espresso stops being mysterious and
    becomes a sequence of small, readable adjustments.

keyPoint: >-
  Fix the dose and the yield, then change only the grind. Moving three variables at once is
  the single most common reason espresso feels impossible to learn.

equipmentEssential:
  - name: Espresso machine
    note: It needs to hold brew pressure and temperature steadily enough to repeat a shot.
  - name: Espresso-capable grinder
    note: The finest setting on a filter grinder is usually still too coarse. This is the part that matters most.
  - name: Portafilter and basket
    note: Use the basket the machine came with and dose to its intended capacity.
  - name: Tamper
    note: It must match the basket diameter. A loose tamper leaves an uneven edge.
  - name: Digital scale
    note: Reading to 0.1 g. You weigh the dose going in and the espresso coming out.
  - name: Timer
    note: The one on the machine, your scale or your phone. Consistency matters, not precision.

equipmentOptional:
  - name: Distribution tool
    note: A thin-needle tool breaks up clumps before tamping. Helpful with a clumpy grinder.
  - name: Dosing funnel
    note: Keeps coffee in the basket instead of on the counter.
  - name: Tamping mat
    note: A stable surface makes a level tamp far easier to repeat.
  - name: Bottomless portafilter
    note: Shows channelling directly. Diagnostic rather than necessary.
  - name: Milk pitcher
    note: A 350 ml pitcher suits one or two milk drinks.
  - name: Thermometer
    note: Useful while learning where 60°C sits in milk by feel.

howItWorks:
  - >-
    Espresso is pressure brewing. A pump pushes water at around nine bars through a bed of
    coffee that has been ground fine and compressed into a puck. The fine grind creates
    enough resistance that the water cannot simply fall through, and the pressure is what
    overcomes that resistance in a workable amount of time.
  - >-
    That balance between resistance and pressure is the whole method. Grind finer and the
    water meets more resistance, so the shot runs slower and extracts more. Grind coarser
    and it runs faster and extracts less. Dose, tamp and basket size all shift the same
    balance, which is why holding them still makes the grinder a readable control.
  - >-
    Crema is a by-product. Carbon dioxide trapped in the coffee since roasting comes out of
    solution as the pressure drops at the spout, forming a foam. It says something about
    freshness and gas content, but very little about whether the shot tastes good.
  - >-
    The failure mode particular to espresso is channelling. If part of the puck is less
    dense than the rest, water takes that route, over-extracting the easy path and barely
    touching the rest. The result can taste sour and bitter at the same time, at a shot
    time that looks perfectly normal — which is why puck preparation deserves as much
    attention as the grinder setting.

technique:
  - action: Let the machine, group head and portafilter come fully up to temperature.
    why: >-
      Follow the warm-up time in the manual. A cold group pulls heat out of the shot, and a
      cold portafilter does the same. Comparing shots made on a cold and a hot machine tells
      you nothing.
  - action: Purge the group and wipe the basket dry.
    why: >-
      A short flush clears stale water sitting in the group. Water under the coffee makes it
      stick and prepares the puck unevenly.
  - action: Grind and weigh 18 g into the basket.
    why: >-
      Weigh after grinding rather than trusting a timer. Retained grounds and bean density
      both shift what actually lands in the basket.
  - action: Break up clumps and level the bed.
    why: >-
      Uneven density is what causes channelling. A gentle tap or a thin-needle tool is enough;
      you are levelling, not compacting.
  - action: Tamp once, level, until the coffee stops compressing.
    why: >-
      Keep the wrist straight so the tamper stays parallel to the basket. Force matters far
      less than being level and doing the same thing every time.
  - action: Wipe the rim and lock the portafilter in without knocking it.
    why: >-
      Grounds on the rim stop the gasket sealing. Knocking the portafilter after tamping can
      crack the puck away from the basket wall and invite water down the side.
  - action: Put the cup on the scale, tare it and start the pump and timer together.
    why: >-
      One timing convention, used every time. Whether you start at the pump or at the first
      drop matters less than never changing your mind about it.
  - action: Stop the pump at 36 g in the cup, around 25–30 seconds.
    why: >-
      Yield defines the end of the shot, not time. Learn the small offset your machine drips
      after the pump stops and cut it slightly early.
  - action: Stir the shot and taste it once it has cooled for a minute.
    why: >-
      The first and last liquid out are very different, and crema on its own is intensely
      bitter. Stirring gives you the drink you actually made.
  - action: Change the grind by one step and pull again, keeping dose and yield fixed.
    why: >-
      Small changes are readable. If you also move the dose and the yield, the next shot
      cannot tell you which change helped.

variables:
  - name: Grind size
    effect: >-
      Finer grinds slow the shot and raise extraction; coarser grinds speed it up and lower it.
    when: >-
      This is the control you use while dialling in. Change it alone and in single steps.
  - name: Dose
    effect: >-
      More coffee in the basket raises resistance and changes the strength and the headspace
      above the puck.
    when: >-
      Set it once for your basket and leave it. Adjust only if the puck is clearly too shallow
      or touching the shower screen.
  - name: Yield
    effect: >-
      A longer yield extracts more and dilutes the shot; a shorter one concentrates it.
    when: >-
      Adjust after the grind is settled — shorter for a dark roast that turns drying, longer
      for a light roast that stays sour.
  - name: Puck preparation
    effect: >-
      Even distribution and a level tamp decide whether water meets the same resistance across
      the basket.
    when: >-
      Fix this before touching the grinder whenever the shot sprays, runs off to one side, or
      tastes sour and bitter together.
  - name: Water temperature
    effect: >-
      Hotter water extracts faster and helps dense light roasts; cooler water softens a dark
      roast that turns harsh.
    when: >-
      A secondary control. Reach for it only once grind, dose and yield are stable.

tasteAdjustments:
  - result: Sour, thin or sharp
    cause: Under-extraction — the shot ran too fast
    change: Grind one step finer
  - result: Bitter, drying or hollow
    cause: Over-extraction — the shot ran too slowly
    change: Grind one step coarser
  - result: Sour and bitter at the same time
    cause: Channelling — water found an easy path through the puck
    change: Improve distribution and tamp level before changing the grind
  - result: Balanced but too intense
    cause: The concentration is too high for the drink
    change: Extend the yield to 40–42 g at the same dose
  - result: Balanced but weak
    cause: The concentration is too low
    change: Shorten the yield to 30–32 g at the same dose
  - result: Thin crema that disappears at once
    cause: The coffee has lost most of its gas
    change: Use coffee within a few weeks of its roast date

choosingCoffee:
  - >-
    Espresso concentrates everything, including faults, so the coffee matters more here than
    in a long filter brew. Medium and medium-dark roasts are the traditional starting point:
    they are more soluble, extract readily under pressure and give the chocolate, caramel and
    nut character most people associate with the drink.
  - >-
    Light roasts make excellent espresso but they are demanding. Being denser and less
    soluble, they need a finer grind, hotter water and often a longer yield to stop tasting
    sour — expect to spend more shots dialling one in. The reward is fruit and floral clarity
    you will not get from a darker blend.
  - >-
    Blends built for espresso are worth trying precisely because someone has already done
    that balancing work. Some include Robusta deliberately, for body and a denser crema, which
    is a legitimate choice rather than a shortcut. Freshness matters in both directions: very
    fresh coffee still full of gas gives unstable, foamy shots, and coffee months past roasting
    runs fast and tastes flat.

milk:
  - >-
    Milk texturing is two stages. Keep the steam tip just at the surface for the first few
    seconds so small amounts of air enter the milk — the sound is a soft paper tearing rather
    than a splutter. Then raise the pitcher slightly so the tip sits just under the surface,
    off centre, and let the whole volume spin in a whirlpool that folds the air in.
  - >-
    Stop at roughly 55–60°C, or when the base of the pitcher becomes too hot to hold
    comfortably for more than a moment. Milk taken much past 70°C loses sweetness and develops
    a cooked flavour. Wipe and purge the wand immediately, swirl the pitcher until the milk is
    glossy, and pour straight away — textured milk separates within a minute of standing.
  - >-
    If the foam is stiff and bubbly you added air for too long; add less and spin for longer.
    If the milk is hot but flat, the tip went too deep too early. The full technique, including
    what to do with plant milks, is covered in the lesson linked below.

troubleshooting:
  - problem: The shot is sour, thin and runs fast
    cause: Under-extraction — the water passed through too quickly
    first: Grind one step finer, keeping the dose and yield the same
    next: Check the basket is dosed to capacity and the puck is not shallow
  - problem: The shot is bitter, harsh and runs slowly
    cause: Over-extraction, or a puck that is choking the machine
    first: Grind one step coarser
    next: If the first drops take longer than 15 seconds to appear, coarsen again
  - problem: The shot sprays or runs strongly from one side
    cause: Channelling from uneven distribution or a tilted tamp
    first: Level the grounds before tamping and tamp on a stable surface
    next: Stop knocking the portafilter after tamping, which cracks the puck edge
  - problem: There is little or no crema
    cause: Stale beans, too coarse a grind, or water that is not hot enough
    first: Check the roast date — crema fades within a few weeks of roasting
    next: Grind finer and confirm the machine is fully warmed through
  - problem: Shot times vary with no change in the recipe
    cause: Something is being estimated rather than measured, or the puck varies
    first: Weigh the dose and the yield for every shot
    next: Use the same distribution and tamping routine each time
  - problem: The milk will not foam
    cause: The tip is too deep, or the milk is not cold and fresh
    first: Start with cold milk and keep the tip at the surface for the first seconds
    next: If it screams rather than tears paper, raise the pitcher slightly
  - problem: Everything tastes harsh whatever you change
    cause: Old coffee oils in the basket, screen and group
    first: Clean the basket and portafilter and brush the group area
    next: Backflush if the machine supports it, following the manual exactly

cleaning:
  - >-
    After every shot, knock out the puck, rinse the basket and wipe it dry before the next
    dose. Do not leave a spent puck locked in the group: the heat bakes coffee oils onto the
    basket and shower screen, and those oils are the reason a machine starts making every
    coffee taste bitter.
  - >-
    After every milk drink, wipe the steam wand with a dedicated cloth and purge it over the
    drip tray straight away. Milk sets hard within minutes and blocks the tip. Keep that cloth
    separate from the one used on the rest of the machine.
  - >-
    At the end of the day, brush the group and gasket, wash the basket and the metal parts of
    the portafilter, empty the drip tray and wipe the body. Periodically, backflush with water
    or detergent only if your machine has a three-way valve — forcing a blind basket into one
    that does not can damage the pump. Descaling is a separate job with a separate product,
    on a schedule set by your water hardness and the manual.
