intro:
  - >-
    The French press is the least demanding brewer on this site. Coffee and water sit
    together in a jug, a mesh plunger separates them, and that is the whole mechanism.
    There is no pouring technique to learn and no drawdown to manage.
  - >-
    What you get is body. The metal mesh lets oils and some fine particles through, so
    the cup is heavier and rounder than anything a paper filter produces. Flavours blend
    rather than separate, which suits chocolate, nut and caramel character particularly
    well. It also brews several cups at once, which no other method here does comfortably.
  - >-
    The limits are texture and timing. There will always be some sediment at the bottom
    of the cup, and coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting — a press that
    stands on the table for twenty minutes turns bitter regardless of how well it was
    brewed.

keyPoint: >-
  Pour the whole brew out as soon as you have pressed. Coffee left on the grounds carries
  on extracting, and that is what turns a good press bitter by the second cup.

equipmentEssential:
  - name: French press
    note: A 1 litre press suits the 30 g / 500 ml recipe with room to stir.
  - name: Burr grinder

  - name: Digital scale

  - name: Kettle
    note: Any kettle. Pouring control is not part of this method.
  - name: Spoon or stirrer
    note: For wetting the grounds and breaking the crust before pressing.

  - name: Second vessel
    note: Somewhere to decant the brew so it stops extracting.
  - name: Paper filter

  - name: Thermometer

howItWorks:
  - >-
    The French press is full immersion. Every ground is surrounded by water for the whole
    steep, so extraction is even by default — there is no bed for water to channel through
    and no fast or slow route to worry about. That is why the method is so forgiving.
  - >-
    Contact time does the work. With everything submerged, the two things that decide how
    much is extracted are grind size and how long the coffee steeps. Finer grounds have
    more surface area and give more up in the same four minutes; coarser grounds give less.
  - >-
    The plunger is not a filter in the pour-over sense. It is a strainer that holds most of
    the grounds back while letting oils and fine particles through, which is exactly where
    the body comes from. It also does not stop extraction: pressing separates the coffee
    from most of the grounds, but anything still in contact keeps brewing. That is why the
    last step is pouring, not pressing.

technique:
  - action: Boil the kettle and rinse the press with hot water.
    why: >-
      A cold glass jug pulls several degrees out of the brew in the first minute, which is
      enough to leave the cup tasting thin.
  - action: Grind 30 g of coffee at a medium-coarse setting.
    why: >-
      Coarse enough that fines do not flood through the mesh, not so coarse that four
      minutes cannot extract it. Sea salt is a reasonable comparison, not gravel.
  - action: Add the coffee and pour 500 ml of water at 93–96°C.
    why: >-
      Pour with some energy so the water reaches every ground. Dry clumps at the bottom
      simply do not brew.
  - action: Stir once, gently, to wet everything.
    why: >-
      A single stir is enough. Vigorous stirring drives fines through the mesh and adds
      grit to the cup.
  - action: Fit the lid with the plunger raised and steep for four minutes.
    why: >-
      The lid keeps the heat in. Leaving the plunger up means you are not pressing the
      crust down before it has done anything.
  - action: At four minutes, break the crust with a spoon and skim off the foam.
    why: >-
      The crust holds trapped gas and some of the harshest material in the brew. Breaking
      it also lets the grounds start sinking.
  - action: Wait a further two minutes without pressing.
    why: >-
      Most of the grounds settle in this window. Settled grounds mean less sediment and a
      far easier press.
  - action: Press the plunger down slowly and evenly.
    why: >-
      Slow pressure keeps the bed intact. Forcing it stirs the settled grounds straight
      back into the coffee.
  - action: Pour the entire brew out immediately.
    why: >-
      This is the step people skip. Whatever stays in the jug keeps extracting and will be
      noticeably more bitter by the time you go back for it.

variables:
  - name: Grind size
    effect: >-
      Finer grinds give a heavier, stronger cup and more sediment; coarser grinds give a
      lighter, cleaner one.
    when: >-
      Change this first when the cup is thin and sour, or heavy and drying.
  - name: Steep time
    effect: >-
      Longer steeps extract more — more sweetness at first, then more bitterness.
    when: >-
      Adjust when the grind is right but the cup sits slightly under or over.
  - name: Ratio
    effect: >-
      30 g to 500 ml is a balanced start. More coffee makes it stronger without changing
      how it extracts.
    when: >-
      Use it when the flavour is right but the strength is not.
  - name: Water temperature
    effect: >-
      Hotter water extracts faster in an immersion brew, where the coffee cannot escape it.
    when: >-
      Come down towards 93°C when a dark roast tastes harsh at a sensible grind.

tasteAdjustments:
  - result: Sour, thin or watery
    cause: Under-extraction — the grind is too coarse for four minutes
    change: Grind one step finer
  - result: Bitter or drying
    cause: Over-extraction, often from a long steep or coffee left in the jug
    change: Grind one step coarser and pour the brew out straight away
  - result: Gritty in the mouth
    cause: Too many fine particles passing the mesh
    change: Grind coarser and press more slowly
  - result: Too strong, but balanced
    cause: The concentration is too high
    change: Use 450 ml of coffee to the same dose, or top the cup with hot water
  - result: Too weak, but balanced
    cause: The concentration is too low
    change: Increase the dose to 33–35 g at the same water
  - result: The second cup is worse than the first
    cause: The brew stayed on the grounds between pours
    change: Decant the whole press into a jug or thermos immediately

choosingCoffee:
  - >-
    The French press rewards coffee with sweetness and body. Medium and medium-dark roasts
    are the natural fit — chocolate, nut, caramel and dried fruit character all come through
    clearly, and the heavier texture gives them something to sit on.
  - >-
    Light roasts work, but they are not the method's strength. The oils and fines that make
    the cup rich also blur the delicate floral and citrus notes that a lighter coffee is
    usually bought for. If you want that clarity, a pour-over will show it better.
  - >-
    Very dark, oily roasts extract quickly and can turn bitter within the standard four
    minutes. Go a step coarser, use water at the lower end, and taste at three and a half
    minutes before committing. For milk drinks the press is workable but not ideal: brew it
    stronger, around 35 g to 450 ml, so it is still recognisable under milk.

troubleshooting:
  - problem: The cup is full of sludge
    cause: The grind is too fine, or the coffee was stirred hard before pressing
    first: Move the grinder two steps coarser
    next: Skim the crust, wait for the grounds to settle, then press slowly
  - problem: The plunger is very hard to push
    cause: The grind is too fine and has packed against the mesh
    first: Grind coarser
    next: Check the mesh is seated flat and the filter discs are not clogged
  - problem: The coffee tastes weak no matter what
    cause: The dose is low, or the grind is far too coarse
    first: Weigh the dose — 30 g to 500 ml is the reference
    next: Grind finer in single steps until the body fills out
  - problem: The brew tastes bitter by the second cup
    cause: The coffee stayed in contact with the grounds
    first: Pour the entire press out as soon as you have plunged
    next: Keep the rest in a warmed jug or thermos rather than in the press
  - problem: The coffee goes cold quickly
    cause: A cold glass jug and a large surface area lose heat fast
    first: Rinse the press with boiling water before brewing
    next: Decant into a preheated thermos if you drink it over a longer stretch
  - problem: Grounds float and never settle
    cause: Very fresh coffee is still releasing gas
    first: Break the crust at four minutes and give it the full two-minute rest
    next: Let the coffee sit a few days longer after roasting before brewing it this way

cleaning:
  - >-
    After each brew, empty the grounds and rinse every part, including the mesh. Wet grounds
    left in the jug are the fastest way to make the next brew taste stale, and they dry into
    a crust that takes real effort to shift.
  - >-
    Once a week, unscrew the plunger assembly completely. The mesh, the cross plate and the
    spiral guard all trap oils where you cannot see them, and that is where a rancid, oily
    smell comes from. Wash the parts in warm soapy water and dry them before reassembling.
  - >-
    Check the mesh for distortion when you reassemble it. A bent or slack screen lets far more
    grounds past, which is usually blamed on the grinder. Replacement mesh discs are cheap and
    restore the cup more effectively than any change to the recipe.
