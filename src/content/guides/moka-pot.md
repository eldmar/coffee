intent: "I want strong coffee without a machine"

notice: >-
  The moka pot is a pressure vessel. Never block, cover or tamp over the safety valve, and
  never open the pot while it is hot.

intro:
  - >-
    The moka pot is the stovetop brewer that put strong coffee in European kitchens for
    most of a century. Water in the base is heated until steam pressure pushes it up
    through a basket of coffee and out into the upper chamber. No pump, no filter paper,
    no electricity.
  - >-
    What comes out is concentrated and full of body, with the roast character well
    forward. It is excellent with warm milk, which is how a great deal of it is drunk,
    and it makes two small cups in about five minutes from a cold start.
  - >-
    It is worth being clear about what it is not. A moka pot works at roughly one to two
    bars, against the nine an espresso machine produces. The coffee is strong and syrupy,
    but it is not espresso and will not build the same crema. The other constraint is
    heat: this method punishes a hot stove more than any other on the site.

keyPoint: >-
  Take the pot off the heat when the flow turns pale and starts to hiss. Everything after
  that point is the bitter, over-extracted end of the brew.

equipmentEssential:
  - name: Moka pot
    note: A 3-cup pot suits the 18 g recipe. Brew the size you own — a half-filled basket brews badly.
  - name: Burr grinder
    note: Medium-fine and even. Espresso-fine grounds will choke the basket and raise the pressure.
  - name: Digital scale
    note: Filling the basket by eye works, but weighing it makes a bad brew diagnosable.
  - name: Heat source
    note: A hob small enough that the flame or ring does not reach past the base.
  - name: Kettle
    note: For preheating the water, which is the single biggest improvement to this method.

equipmentOptional:
  - name: Cloth or oven glove
    note: The base gets genuinely hot. Assembling with preheated water needs one.
  - name: Timer
    note: Useful while you learn how your hob and pot behave together.
  - name: Spare gasket
    note: A wear part. A hardened gasket leaks steam and weakens the brew.
  - name: Milk pitcher
    note: If most of your moka coffee ends up as a milk drink.

howItWorks:
  - >-
    The moka pot brews with steam pressure. Water in the sealed lower chamber is heated;
    as it warms, the air and steam above it expand and push down on the surface of the
    water. The only route out is up the funnel, through the coffee basket and into the
    upper chamber.
  - >-
    That gives you a brew driven by heat rather than by time. There is no steeping and no
    pour to control. How fast the water is forced through the coffee depends on how hard
    you heat the base, and how much resistance the coffee bed offers.
  - >-
    This explains the two rules that matter. Grind medium-fine, not espresso-fine: too much
    resistance sends the pressure up rather than the water through. And keep the heat low
    to medium, because a fierce flame pushes water through faster than the coffee can give
    up anything pleasant, and it heats the empty base towards the end of the brew — which
    is where the burnt note comes from.
  - >-
    Starting with preheated water shortens the time the pot spends heating up, so the
    coffee in the basket is not sitting over a warming chamber for several minutes before
    the brew begins.

technique:
  - action: Boil the kettle and fill the base with hot water up to the safety valve.
    why: >-
      Never above the valve. Preheated water means the pot reaches brewing pressure quickly
      instead of slowly stewing the grounds.
  - action: Grind 18 g of coffee at a medium-fine setting.
    why: >-
      Finer than filter, coarser than espresso. Too fine and the pressure climbs instead of
      the coffee flowing.
  - action: Fill the basket level and full, then tap it once to settle.
    why: >-
      Do not tamp. A tamped bed in a moka pot restricts flow and raises pressure, which is
      the opposite of what this brewer wants.
  - action: Brush loose grounds off the rim and screw the pot together firmly.
    why: >-
      Grounds on the rim stop the gasket sealing, and steam that escapes sideways never
      reaches the coffee. Use a cloth — the base is hot.
  - action: Put it on a low to medium heat with the lid open.
    why: >-
      The open lid is how you see the brew. Low heat gives you time to react before the
      pot runs away with itself.
  - action: Wait for the coffee to rise in a steady, dark stream.
    why: >-
      A calm honey-coloured flow means the pressure is right. A violent spurt means the
      heat is too high; turn it down rather than starting again.
  - action: Take the pot off the heat as the flow turns pale and begins to hiss.
    why: >-
      The pale liquid is the weak, bitter end of the extraction, and the hiss is steam
      arriving where coffee used to be.
  - action: Cool the base briefly under a running tap or on a damp cloth.
    why: >-
      Optional, but it stops the brew immediately instead of letting residual heat push
      the last harsh liquid through.
  - action: Stir the upper chamber and pour straight away.
    why: >-
      The first and last coffee out are very different in strength. Stirring gives you an
      honest cup, and pouring promptly keeps it off the hot metal.

variables:
  - name: Heat level
    effect: >-
      Low to medium heat gives a steady flow and a sweeter cup. High heat forces the water
      through and adds burnt, bitter notes.
    when: >-
      This is the first thing to change when the coffee tastes scorched or sputters violently.
  - name: Grind size
    effect: >-
      Finer grinds raise resistance and pressure; coarser grinds let water through faster and
      weaken the brew.
    when: >-
      Adjust when the pot struggles and spits, or when the coffee runs through thin and quickly.
  - name: Water temperature at the start
    effect: >-
      Preheated water shortens the warm-up, so the coffee is not baked above a heating base.
    when: >-
      Switch to it if the cup tastes stewed or vaguely burnt even on a low flame.
  - name: When you stop the brew
    effect: >-
      Stopping at the pale stage keeps the cup sweet. Letting it run to a full sputter adds
      the harshest liquid.
    when: >-
      Change this before touching the grind if the last mouthful is noticeably worse than the first.

tasteAdjustments:
  - result: Burnt or scorched
    cause: The heat was too high, or the pot stayed on after the brew finished
    change: Lower the heat and take the pot off as the flow turns pale
  - result: Bitter and harsh
    cause: Over-extraction, usually a grind that is too fine for the pressure
    change: Grind one step coarser
  - result: Sour and thin
    cause: Under-extraction — the water passed through too easily
    change: Grind one step finer and check the basket is filled level and full
  - result: Weak, with a lot of liquid
    cause: The basket was underfilled for the pot size
    change: Brew the size the pot is built for, filling the basket completely
  - result: Steam escapes from the seam
    cause: The gasket is worn, or grounds on the rim are breaking the seal
    change: Wipe the rim, tighten firmly, and replace the gasket if it is hard or cracked
  - result: Coffee spurts violently
    cause: Too much heat, too fine a grind, or both
    change: Turn the heat down first, then coarsen the grind if it continues

choosingCoffee:
  - >-
    Medium and medium-dark roasts are the traditional choice and the easiest to get right.
    The method concentrates whatever is in the coffee, so chocolate, caramel, nut and
    gentle spice character all come through with plenty of weight behind them.
  - >-
    Light roasts are harder work here. They are dense and less soluble, and the moka pot
    does not offer the temperature control to coax them open — the result is often sharp
    rather than bright. If you want to try one, grind slightly finer and keep the heat low.
  - >-
    Very dark, oily beans are popular for moka and do give the classic intense cup, but they
    are also the quickest to turn bitter. Watch the flow closely and stop early. For milk
    drinks, a medium-dark roast is the safest choice: it stays recognisable under warm milk
    without needing to be brewed to the point of harshness.

troubleshooting:
  - problem: The coffee tastes burnt
    cause: The heat was too high, or the pot stayed on the hob after brewing
    first: Turn the heat down to low or medium and use preheated water
    next: Remove the pot as soon as the stream pales and cool the base
  - problem: Nothing comes up, or barely a trickle
    cause: The grind is too fine, or the basket was tamped
    first: Grind coarser and fill the basket level without pressing
    next: Check the filter plate and gasket are not blocked with old grounds
  - problem: The pot spits and sputters loudly from the start
    cause: The heat is too high for the pot
    first: Turn the heat down — the flame should not spread past the base
    next: If it continues, coarsen the grind by one step
  - problem: Steam leaks around the middle seam
    cause: A worn gasket, or grounds trapped on the rim
    first: Wipe the rim clean and tighten the two halves firmly
    next: Replace the gasket if it is hardened, shiny or cracked
  - problem: The coffee is weak and watery
    cause: The basket was not full, or the grind is too coarse
    first: Fill the basket completely and level for the pot size you own
    next: Grind one step finer and check the pot matches the amount you want to brew
  - problem: There is no crema at all
    cause: A moka pot brews at one to two bars and does not build espresso crema
    first: Judge the brew on sweetness and body rather than the surface
    next: Use fresher coffee, which produces a little more foam, but expect no more than that

cleaning:
  - >-
    After each brew, let the pot cool, then empty the basket and rinse every part with hot
    water. Traditional advice says never to use soap on aluminium; the more useful point is
    to rinse thoroughly and dry properly, because trapped moisture is what corrodes the
    metal and stales the next cup.
  - >-
    Dry all three parts separately and store the pot unscrewed. Screwing a damp pot together
    traps moisture against the gasket and the seam, and that is where both the smell and the
    corrosion begin.
  - >-
    Check the gasket and the metal filter plate regularly. The gasket hardens with heat and
    stops sealing, and the small holes in the plate clog with old grounds — hold it up to a
    light and clear any that are blocked. Both parts are inexpensive and both restore the
    brew more effectively than changing coffee. Inspect the safety valve at the same time and
    keep it clear.
