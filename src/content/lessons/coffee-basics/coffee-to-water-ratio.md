## The short answer

For filter and immersion coffee, start with **60 g of coffee per litre of water**. That is the same as a ratio of approximately **1:16.7**: one gram of coffee for every 16.7 grams of water.

It is a starting point, not a law. The useful part is that a ratio gives you a recipe you can repeat.

</div>

## What a coffee ratio means

A ratio compares the mass of dry coffee with the mass of brewing water.

A `1:16` ratio means:

- 1 g coffee to 16 g water;
- 15 g coffee to 240 g water;
- 30 g coffee to 480 g water.

Because one millilitre of water weighs almost one gram, weighing water is easier and more accurate than reading lines on a jug.

## A simple starting table

| Brewing water | Coffee at 1:16.7 |
| ---: | ---: |
| 200 g | 12 g |
| 250 g | 15 g |
| 300 g | 18 g |
| 500 g | 30 g |
| 750 g | 45 g |
| 1,000 g | 60 g |

To calculate the coffee dose:

`water ÷ ratio = coffee`

For example:

`300 g water ÷ 16.7 = 18 g coffee`

## Ratio changes strength

Using more coffee with the same amount of water usually creates a stronger drink. Using less coffee usually creates a weaker one.

Common starting ranges for brewed coffee are:

- **1:15:** stronger and more concentrated;
- **1:16 to 1:17:** balanced starting range;
- **1:18:** lighter and less concentrated.

Strength is not the same as extraction. A coffee can be strong and sour if the water has not extracted enough from the grounds. It can also be weak and bitter if a small amount of coffee has been over-extracted.

Use ratio mainly to set strength. Use grind size, time, temperature and agitation to manage extraction.

## Espresso uses a different ratio

For espresso, we normally compare the dry coffee dose with the mass of liquid espresso in the cup. This is often called a beverage ratio.

A common starting recipe is:

- 18 g coffee in;
- 36 g espresso out;
- a ratio of 1:2.

Unlike a filter recipe, the second number refers to the finished beverage, not all the water used by the machine.

Typical espresso ranges include:

- **Ristretto:** approximately 1:1 to 1:1.5;
- **Espresso:** approximately 1:2;
- **Lungo:** approximately 1:2.5 to 1:3.

The labels are useful, but taste should still make the final decision.

## What about French press and AeroPress?

The same 1:16 to 1:17 starting range works well for many French press recipes.

AeroPress recipes vary more widely. Some brew at a normal filter ratio, while others make a concentrated coffee and dilute it afterwards. Follow the recipe first, then adjust it deliberately.

## How to adjust a ratio

If the coffee tastes balanced but is simply too strong or too weak:

- **Too strong:** use slightly more water or slightly less coffee.
- **Too weak:** use slightly less water or slightly more coffee.

Change the ratio in small steps. Moving from 1:16 to 1:16.5 is easier to understand than jumping directly to 1:19.

If the coffee is sour, bitter, dry or hollow, do not assume ratio is the only problem. Grind size is often the more useful next adjustment.

<div class="callout callout--exercise">

## Try this today

Brew two small cups with the same coffee and grind size:

- Cup A: 15 g coffee and 240 g water — 1:16.
- Cup B: 15 g coffee and 270 g water — 1:18.

Taste them side by side. Do not ask which one is objectively correct. Ask which strength makes the flavours easier for you to enjoy.

</div>

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Start around 60 g per litre, weigh both coffee and water, and treat the ratio as your strength control. Once the strength feels right, use grind size to improve balance.

</div>
