## The short answer

Taste coffee when it is warm rather than painfully hot. Compare it with another cup and describe five things: sweetness, acidity, bitterness, body and aftertaste. Ordinary words are completely acceptable.

</div>

## Tasting is attention, not performance

Coffee tasting can sound unnecessarily theatrical. A bag mentions peach, jasmine and cocoa nibs; you taste coffee and begin to suspect you have failed an exam nobody told you about.

Flavour notes are comparisons, not added ingredients and not a test with one correct answer. They describe what the coffee reminds someone of.

Your first job is not to find blueberries. It is to notice whether the cup is enjoyable and how it differs from another one.

## Let the coffee cool

Begin with the aroma while the coffee is hot, but do not make the final judgement immediately. As the drink cools, sweetness, acidity and defects often become easier to recognise.

Taste at three stages:

1. hot;
2. comfortably warm;
3. close to room temperature.

A good coffee should not collapse the moment it cools.

## Five useful questions

### 1. Is it sweet?

Coffee contains no obvious sugar unless you add it, but well-extracted coffee can suggest caramel, ripe fruit, chocolate or honey-like sweetness.

Ask whether the cup feels naturally rounded or whether bitterness and acidity dominate everything else.

### 2. What is the acidity like?

Acidity is not automatically sourness. Pleasant acidity can feel lively, juicy or crisp, like apple, citrus or berries.

Unpleasant sourness often feels sharp, empty or unfinished. It may be a brewing problem rather than a feature of the coffee.

### 3. How bitter is it?

All coffee contains some bitterness. The question is whether it supports the other flavours or dominates the cup.

Pleasant bitterness may suggest dark chocolate or grapefruit peel. Unpleasant bitterness may feel burnt, medicinal or aggressively dry.

### 4. What is the body like?

Body is the physical weight and texture of the drink.

- Tea-like or light;
- silky or smooth;
- round or creamy;
- heavy or syrupy;
- rough or drying.

French press coffee often has more body than paper-filtered pour-over because more oils and fine particles remain in the cup.

### 5. What remains afterwards?

Aftertaste is what stays once you swallow.

Is the finish sweet and clean? Does it disappear quickly? Does a dry bitterness remain? A long aftertaste is not automatically good—the quality of what remains matters more than its duration.

## Comparison makes tasting easier

Tasting one coffee alone asks your brain to remember an abstract standard. Tasting two cups side by side gives it a direct contrast.

Compare:

- the same coffee at two grind settings;
- tap water and filtered water;
- two different origins;
- a light roast and a dark roast;
- the same coffee hot and cool.

Differences that seemed invisible in isolation often become obvious in pairs.

## A simple tasting note

Use this format:

> Sweetness: low / medium / high  
> Acidity: soft / bright / sharp  
> Bitterness: low / balanced / dominant  
> Body: light / smooth / heavy  
> Aftertaste: clean / short / dry  
> Overall: what would I change next time?

Specific flavour notes can come later. "Sweet, bright and tea-like" is already useful information.

## Slurping is optional

Professional tasters slurp coffee to spread it across the mouth and carry aroma towards the nose. You can try it with a spoon, but it is not required for paying attention to a cup at home.

Taste in whatever way helps you compare consistently. The neighbours do not need to hear the assessment.

<div class="callout callout--exercise">

## Try this today

Brew one coffee and divide it between two cups. Taste the first cup immediately and wait ten minutes before tasting the second.

Write down only three words for each cup. Notice how the description changes with temperature.

</div>

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Taste slowly, compare cups and use language that makes sense to you. The goal is not to sound like an expert; it is to understand what you enjoy and what to adjust.
