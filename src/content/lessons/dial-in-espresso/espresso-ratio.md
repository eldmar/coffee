## The short answer

Espresso ratio compares the mass of dry coffee in the basket with the mass of liquid espresso in the cup. With an 18 g dose, **1:2 means 36 g out**, **1:2.5 means 45 g out** and **1:3 means 54 g out**.

Start around 1:2 for a balanced medium roast. Use a shorter ratio for more concentration or a darker roast, and a longer ratio when a light roast needs more extraction. Ratio sets the target yield; it does not tell you by itself whether the grind is correct.

</div>

## What an Espresso Ratio Means

The first number represents dry coffee dose and the second represents beverage yield. A 1:2 recipe produces two grams of espresso for every gram of ground coffee:

> 18 g dose × 2 = 36 g espresso yield

This is a brew ratio, not the total amount of water used by the machine. Water remains in the puck, enters the drip tray and moves through internal plumbing. Espresso recipes use only the coffee placed in the basket and the beverage collected in the cup.

Weigh both. Basket markings, grinder timers and cup volume are too imprecise for comparing small changes.

## Dose vs Beverage Yield

**Dose** is the dry ground coffee in the basket. Choose a dose that suits the basket and gives enough headspace below the shower screen. Keep it fixed while comparing ratios.

**Beverage yield** is the liquid espresso in the cup. Measure it in grams because crema changes volume without adding the same mass. Stop the shot when the scale reaches the target rather than when the cup looks full.

The [Espresso basics lesson](/learn/dial-in-espresso/espresso-basics-dose-yield-time/) explains how dose, yield and time form one repeatable recipe. The calculator above handles the arithmetic so you can concentrate on taste.

## Espresso Ratio Comparison

| Ratio | Yield from 18 g | Typical style | Concentration | Useful starting point |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 1:1–1:1.5 | 18–27 g | Ristretto | Very high | Dark roast or a short, dense shot |
| 1:2 | 36 g | Standard espresso | High | Balanced medium roast |
| 1:2.5 | 45 g | Modern espresso | Medium-high | Light to medium roast needing more extraction |
| 1:3 | 54 g | Longer ratio | Medium | Very light roast or a more open, tea-like shot |

These are descriptions, not fixed definitions. The coffee’s solubility, basket, machine and desired drink all affect the best stopping point.

## 1:1–1:1.5 Ristretto

A ristretto uses roughly the same mass of beverage as dry coffee, or up to one-and-a-half times the dose. An 18 g dose therefore yields about 18–27 g in the cup.

The result is dense, syrupy and highly concentrated. Short yield can soften the late bitterness of a dark roast, but it also reduces total extraction. A light roast may taste sharp, salty or undeveloped when stopped this early.

Do not make a ristretto simply by grinding so fine that the machine barely moves water. Choose the target yield first, then adjust grind for an even flow within a sensible time.

## 1:2 Standard Espresso

A 1:2 ratio is the most useful neutral starting point. With 18 g coffee, stop at 36 g espresso. Many medium roasts balance sweetness, body and extraction near this range.

It also creates a clear baseline for dialling in. Keep 18 g in and 36 g out while adjusting the grind to bring flow and taste into balance. Once the shot is even and repeatable, change the ratio only when the flavour suggests that the stopping point itself is wrong.

Use the [Espresso recipe](/recipes/espresso/) for a complete 1:2 workflow, including puck preparation and timing.

## 1:2.5 Modern Espresso

A 1:2.5 ratio produces 45 g espresso from an 18 g dose. The extra yield gives water more opportunity to extract sweetness and flavour from a dense light or medium roast.

The shot is less concentrated than 1:2, so it may feel lighter while tasting more developed. That distinction matters: stronger is not the same as better extracted.

Move from 1:2 towards 1:2.5 when a well-prepared shot runs in a reasonable time but remains sour, tight or underdeveloped. Keep dose, temperature and puck preparation constant so the longer yield is the only intentional recipe change.

## 1:3 Longer Ratio

A 1:3 ratio yields 54 g from 18 g coffee. It can suit very light roasts that need more extraction or coffees whose floral and fruit character becomes clearer at lower concentration.

Longer ratios can also expose dryness. If the final part of the shot tastes woody, hollow or astringent, stop earlier rather than chasing a number. A 1:3 target should express the coffee, not become a test of how much liquid the puck can surrender.

This ratio remains espresso, but its texture may feel closer to a small, intense filter coffee than a traditional syrupy shot.

## How Roast Level Affects Ratio

Dark roasts are more soluble and release flavour quickly. They often suit 1:1.5–1:2, where a shorter yield protects body and avoids pulling too much late bitterness.

Medium roasts commonly work around 1:2, with movement towards 1:2.5 when more sweetness is needed. Light roasts are denser and less soluble, so 1:2.5–1:3 may reveal more balanced acidity and aroma.

These are starting points. The [coffee roast levels guide](/learn/understand-your-beans/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) explains why colour alone cannot predict every extraction.

## How Ratio Changes Taste

Increasing yield usually does two things at once: it extracts more material from the coffee and lowers beverage concentration. A 1:2.5 shot can therefore taste sweeter and more developed but feel lighter than 1:2.

Decreasing yield raises concentration and stops extraction earlier. The shot may feel heavier and more intense, but can become sour or salty when the coffee needed more contact.

Use plain sensory language:

- sour, sharp or underdeveloped may benefit from a slightly longer ratio;
- dry, woody or hollow may benefit from a slightly shorter ratio;
- balanced but too intense may need more yield or dilution, depending on the drink;
- balanced but too light may need a shorter ratio.

Change in small steps. For an 18 g dose, two or three grams of yield can be enough to reveal the direction.

## Change Ratio or Grind Size?

Use **grind size** when flow is clearly too fast or too slow, or when the target ratio is not reached in a workable time. Keep dose and yield fixed while adjusting grind so the result remains diagnosable.

Use **ratio** when flow and puck preparation look sensible but the taste suggests the shot should stop earlier or continue longer. Keep grind and dose fixed for that test.

Time is evidence, not an additional recipe target to change independently. Grinding finer may lengthen time; that longer time is the consequence of the grind change.

The [Espresso brew guide](/guides/espresso/) covers the full workflow. For a taste-led next step, use the Brew Assistant rather than changing grind and ratio together.

## Practical Dial-In Examples

**18 g in, 36 g out, 20 seconds, sour:** keep the 1:2 yield and grind slightly finer. The shot is reaching the target too quickly, so ratio is not the first variable to move.

**18 g in, 36 g out, 29 seconds, still sour:** try 40–45 g out with the same dose and grind. Flow is already plausible, so a longer ratio tests whether the coffee needs more extraction.

**18 g in, 45 g out, 31 seconds, dry finish:** stop at 40–42 g while keeping dose and grind fixed. The late part of the shot may be adding astringency.

**20 g basket with a preferred 1:2.5 ratio:** target 50 g yield. The calculator scales by dose, so you do not need to copy an 18 g recipe into a basket designed for more coffee.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best espresso ratio?

Start around 1:2 for a medium roast, then use taste to move shorter or longer. Dark roasts often suit 1:1.5–1:2, while light roasts may benefit from 1:2.5–1:3.

### How do you calculate espresso yield?

Multiply the dry coffee dose by the second number in the ratio. For example, 18 g at 1:2.5 gives a target beverage yield of 45 g.

### Is a 1:3 ratio still espresso?

Yes. It is a longer, less concentrated espresso ratio. The texture may be lighter, but it is still produced through an espresso puck under pressure.

### Does a longer ratio contain more caffeine?

It may extract slightly more caffeine from the same dose, but the difference is smaller than changing the coffee dose or number of shots. A longer yield mainly changes extraction and concentration.

### Should I change ratio or grind first?

Change grind first when the shot reaches its target far too quickly or slowly. Change ratio when flow is sensible but taste suggests the shot should stop earlier or continue longer. Change only one controlled variable per test.

### Does crema count in espresso yield?

Yes. Weigh the full beverage, including crema, on a scale. Crema makes volume unreliable, but its mass is already included in the gram reading.

Ratio defines where the shot stops. Use 1:2 as a clear baseline, move longer or shorter according to coffee and taste, and keep grind changes separate so each shot teaches you something.
