## The short answer

**Sour espresso** feels sharply acidic, thin or unfinished. It often makes the mouth water quickly and may disappear without much sweetness. It commonly points to low or uneven extraction.

**Bitter espresso** tastes harsh, dark or burnt. **Astringent espresso** leaves the mouth dry, like over-brewed tea. These qualities can point to excessive or uneven extraction, a roast style you do not enjoy, or dirty equipment.

Acidity is not automatically a fault, and bitterness is not automatically over-extraction. Use taste together with dose, yield, time and flow before changing the recipe.

</div>

## Acidity is not the same as sourness

Good acidity gives coffee structure. It can taste bright, juicy or fruit-like and should sit alongside sweetness. Sourness feels less integrated: sharp at the front of the tongue, hollow through the middle and short in the finish.

A light-roasted washed coffee may naturally have more acidity than a dark espresso blend. Trying to remove every trace of acidity can also remove the character that made the coffee interesting.

Ask a better question than "Is it acidic?":

> Does the acidity feel sweet and complete, or sharp and empty?

## Bitterness and astringency are different

Bitterness is a taste. Dark chocolate, tonic water and some roasted foods are bitter. Espresso naturally contains some bitterness, and a small amount can balance sweetness and acidity.

Astringency is a drying sensation. The gums, tongue or inner cheeks may feel rough after swallowing. It often appears when extraction is uneven: water over-extracts some areas of the puck while bypassing others.

This is why one shot can taste sour and drying at the same time. The average shot time may look normal while different parts of the puck behaved very differently.

## Common signs in the cup

| What you notice | Possible interpretation | First check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sharp, thin, salty or quickly sour | Low extraction | Was the shot fast? Try finer |
| Bright but sweet, clear and lingering | Pleasant acidity | Do not fix what is not broken |
| Heavy, harsh, bitter and slow | High extraction or too much contact | Try coarser |
| Dry finish that grips the mouth | Astringency or channeling | Inspect puck preparation and flow |
| Sour and bitter together | Uneven extraction | Improve distribution before changing the recipe |
| Burnt or smoky at every setting | Roast character, excessive temperature or dirty equipment | Check the coffee, temperature and cleanliness |

These are clues, not diagnoses. Taste descriptions overlap, and different people perceive acidity and bitterness differently.

## How to correct a sour shot

First confirm the recipe. If the target yield arrived much too quickly, grind finer and repeat the same dose and yield.

If the time is already sensible and flow looks even:

1. Try a slightly finer grind.
2. If the machine begins to choke, return to the previous grind and extend the yield a little—for example, from 1:2 to 1:2.2.
3. If the coffee is a light roast and the machine allows temperature control, try a slightly higher brew temperature.

Make one change at a time. A longer yield and finer grind both tend to increase extraction, but changing both together makes the result harder to understand.

## How to correct a bitter or drying shot

If the target yield takes far too long, grind coarser.

If time and flow look reasonable:

1. Try a slightly shorter yield.
2. Check for spurting, side-heavy flow or holes in the puck that suggest channeling.
3. Clean the basket, shower screen and group area.
4. If the coffee is very dark, consider whether its roast character is simply darker and more bitter than you prefer.

Lowering temperature can help some dark roasts, but do not use temperature as the first adjustment when grind and flow are clearly wrong.

## Stir before judging

Espresso develops in layers during extraction. The early part is more concentrated; the later part is thinner, and crema can be intensely bitter. Stir the shot before tasting so you judge the complete drink.

Let it cool for a minute as well. Extreme heat hides detail. A balanced shot often becomes sweeter and clearer as it cools, while faults become easier to name.

## Build your own taste reference

Words become useful through comparison. Pull one intentionally fast shot, one normal shot and one intentionally slow shot using the same dose and yield. Taste them side by side.

Do not drink all three unless you have made ambitious plans for the ceiling. Small sips are enough.

<div class="callout callout--exercise">

## Try this today

Stir a fresh espresso and take three small sips: immediately, after one minute and after three minutes. For each sip, record sweetness, acidity, bitterness, body and dryness using a low–medium–high scale.

This gives you a clearer vocabulary than simply writing "bad".

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Sourness often suggests low extraction, harsh bitterness may suggest excessive extraction and dryness often points to uneven flow. Acidity and bitterness can also belong in a balanced espresso, so use taste, recipe and flow together.
