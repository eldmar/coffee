## The short answer

Arabica and Robusta are coffees from different plant species. **Arabica** means *Coffea arabica*. **Robusta** is the common trade name for *Coffea canephora*.

Arabica often tastes more aromatic, sweet and acidic. Robusta often has more bitterness, heavier body and more caffeine. Those are broad tendencies, not quality rankings. Excellent Canephora exists, poor Arabica exists, and the way coffee is grown, processed, roasted and brewed can matter more than the species name on the bag.

</div>

## Two species, not two roast styles

Coffee belongs to a large group of plant species, but Arabica and Canephora account for most coffee sold around the world.

Arabica developed from a natural relationship between ancestors related to Canephora and *Coffea eugenioides*. It has less genetic diversity than Canephora and is generally more vulnerable to heat, pests and diseases.

Canephora is genetically diverse and includes many populations and cultivated types. "Robusta" became the familiar commercial name, but it does not describe every Canephora in the same way.

This matters because neither category is one single flavour. An SL28 Arabica from Kenya, a Bourbon from El Salvador and a low-grown commercial Arabica blend can taste dramatically different. Fine Canephora lots can also be far cleaner and sweeter than the rubbery stereotype suggests.

## How they tend to differ

| Characteristic | Arabica | Robusta / Canephora |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common cup profile | More aromatic, sweet, bright or fruit-led | More bitter, full-bodied, earthy, woody or cocoa-led |
| Caffeine | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| Growing conditions | Often cooler and higher, though not always | Often warmer and lower, with greater heat tolerance |
| Plant resilience | More vulnerable to several diseases and climate pressures | Often more robust, but not invulnerable |
| Common use | Single origins, filter coffee, espresso and blends | Espresso blends, instant coffee and increasingly fine Canephora lots |
| Typical price | Often higher | Often lower, with major exceptions at the quality end |

The word "often" is doing important work in this table.

## What changes in the cup

Arabica commonly provides more perceived acidity and a wider range of floral, fruit, caramel and nut-like associations. That is one reason speciality coffee has historically concentrated on Arabica.

Robusta commonly contributes:

- heavier body;
- stronger bitterness;
- darker cocoa, cereal, spice, wood or earthy notes;
- more caffeine;
- dense, persistent crema in espresso blends.

Traditional Italian-style espresso blends may use Robusta deliberately for body, intensity and crema. Its presence is not automatically evidence that someone tried to ruin the coffee before breakfast.

## Does Robusta contain twice the caffeine?

Robusta generally contains considerably more caffeine than Arabica, and "roughly twice as much" is a useful broad description. The exact amount varies with variety, growing conditions, roast, dose and brewing method.

The drink still matters more than the species label alone. A large filter coffee made from Arabica can contain more total caffeine than a small espresso containing some Robusta because it uses more coffee and water.

If caffeine sensitivity matters, compare serving size and recipe rather than relying only on the bean species.

## Is 100% Arabica a quality guarantee?

No. It tells you the species, not whether the cherries were ripe, the processing was careful, the roast was skilful or the coffee is fresh.

"100% Arabica" became a useful supermarket distinction when much lower-cost coffee contained commodity Robusta. It can still help describe a product, but it does not function like a score.

More useful quality clues include:

- a roast date;
- producer, farm, co-operative or regional information;
- variety and processing details;
- a roaster whose coffees you trust;
- flavour notes that match your preferences.

## What is Fine Robusta?

Fine Robusta or Fine Canephora refers to coffees produced and evaluated with greater attention to genetics, harvesting, processing and sensory quality. These lots may show sweetness, clean fruit, spice or chocolate characteristics without the harsh defects associated with poorly handled commodity coffee.

The category is still developing, and quality varies. The useful lesson is that species sets possibilities; it does not write the entire cup profile in advance.

## Which should you buy?

Choose by the drink you want.

- For fragrant filter coffee with fruit, floral or bright flavours, Arabica is the more common starting point.
- For a strong traditional espresso with heavy body and pronounced bitterness, an Arabica–Robusta blend may be exactly right.
- For milk drinks, a well-built blend can keep its character through milk.
- If you find a carefully produced Canephora, try it without expecting it to imitate Arabica.

The most useful comparison is side by side, brewed with the same method at a suitable recipe for each coffee.

## Try this today

Find one 100% Arabica coffee and one blend that lists Robusta or Canephora. Brew or order them at a similar strength. Compare aroma, sweetness, bitterness, body and aftertaste without deciding in advance which one is supposed to be better.

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Arabica and Robusta are different coffee species with different common tendencies. Arabica is not automatically excellent and Robusta is not automatically poor. Species is one useful clue among genetics, origin, processing, roast and brewing.
