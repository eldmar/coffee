## The short answer

Coffee flavour notes are comparisons. If a bag says "blueberry, jasmine and dark chocolate", the roaster is describing aromas, tastes or textures that reminded tasters of those references.

The ingredients are still coffee and water unless the bag explicitly says the coffee is flavoured. Notes are a guide to character, not a promise that the drink will taste like blueberry juice.

</div>

## How coffee can suggest other foods

Coffee contains a complex mixture of aromatic compounds, acids, sugars, bitter compounds and roast products. Our brains connect combinations of aroma, taste and texture with familiar experiences.

A coffee may remind someone of orange because it has bright acidity and citrus-like aroma. It may suggest milk chocolate through sweetness, cocoa aroma and a smooth body. Floral notes often come mainly through aroma rather than a literal flower taste on the tongue.

This is similar to the language used for wine, whisky, tea or chocolate. Description helps people compare experiences that would otherwise be reduced to "coffee-like".

## No blueberries were added

Most speciality coffee with fruit, floral or chocolate notes contains no added flavouring.

Flavoured coffee is a separate product. Vanilla, hazelnut or caramel flavour may be applied after roasting, and the packaging should identify added flavouring.

Processing experiments sometimes involve fruit, spices or other materials during fermentation. When external ingredients are deliberately used, transparent producers and roasters should explain that rather than presenting every flavour as a natural expression of the coffee.

## Where tasting words come from

Roasters may taste coffees through cupping, espresso or filter brewing and record shared descriptors. The Specialty Coffee Association Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel and the World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon provide a common vocabulary and physical references.

A structured language helps one taster's "red fruit" become a more specific comparison such as cherry or raspberry. It does not make sensory perception perfectly objective.

Culture and experience matter. A familiar fruit to one person may be unfamiliar to another. Two trained tasters can agree that a coffee is fruity and sweet but choose different specific examples.

## Start broad, then move closer

Do not begin by searching desperately for white peach.

Ask in stages:

1. Is the coffee mainly fruit-led, nutty, chocolate-led, floral, spicy, roasted or fermented?
2. If fruity, is it citrus, berry, stone fruit, tropical or dried fruit?
3. Which familiar example is closest?

Broad, accurate language is more useful than a highly specific guess made under social pressure.

## The main sensory building blocks

### Aroma

Smell the dry grounds, the wet coffee and the cup as it cools. Much of what we call flavour reaches us through aroma.

### Acidity

Acidity can feel citrus-like, apple-like, juicy or wine-like. Pleasant acidity is integrated with sweetness. Sharp, empty sourness may indicate poor extraction.

### Sweetness

Coffee sweetness is subtler than sugar in tea. It may suggest caramel, ripe fruit, honey or brown sugar.

### Bitterness

Some bitterness belongs in coffee. It can resemble cocoa, tonic water or toasted food. Harsh bitterness may come from roast, extraction or stale residue in equipment.

### Body and texture

Body describes weight and texture: tea-like, silky, creamy, syrupy or heavy. It is not a flavour note, but it changes how flavour is experienced.

### Aftertaste

Notice what remains after swallowing. A clean sweet finish and a dry smoky finish can make two initially similar coffees feel very different.

## Why you may not taste the bag notes

Several reasons are normal:

- the note is subtle;
- your water changes extraction;
- the recipe is not suited to the coffee;
- the coffee is too hot to taste clearly;
- the roast or coffee has aged;
- the note was clearer in cupping than in milk;
- you associate the sensation with a different word.

Milk and sugar also change perception. A berry-led espresso may become more like chocolate or dessert when combined with milk.

## Taste through comparison

One coffee alone is difficult to describe. Two coffees make differences visible.

Brew a washed and natural coffee, or a light and dark roast, then taste them side by side as they cool. You may not find every printed note, but one will feel fruitier, heavier, more floral or more roasted than the other.

Comparison builds sensory memory without requiring professional cupping equipment.

## Are flavour notes marketing?

They are both sensory communication and product positioning. Useful notes set an honest expectation. Overly theatrical notes can create a gap between the bag and the cup.

Judge the note by whether it helps you choose. "Chocolate, caramel and hazelnut" communicates a familiar profile. "A moonlit orchard after summer rain" may communicate that the copywriter had a deadline.

<div class="callout callout--exercise">

## Try this today

Brew one coffee and taste it hot, warm and nearly cool. At each stage, write one broad category, one texture and one aftertaste. Only add a specific flavour reference if it arrives naturally.

</div>

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Flavour notes describe sensory similarities created by the coffee, processing, roast and brewing. Use them as a direction, start with broad categories and do not treat missing one exact note as a failed cup.
