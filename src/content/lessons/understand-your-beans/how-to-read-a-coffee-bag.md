## The short answer

The most useful information on a coffee bag is:

1. roast date;
2. country and region;
3. producer, farm or co-operative;
4. coffee species and variety;
5. processing method;
6. roast level or intended brew method;
7. flavour notes.

You do not need every field to buy good coffee. Look for enough information to understand what you are buying and to find—or avoid—similar coffee next time.

</div>

## Roast date

A roast date tells you when the green coffee became roasted coffee. It is more useful for freshness than a distant best-before date.

Coffee does not peak the moment it leaves the roaster. It releases carbon dioxide after roasting and often becomes easier to brew after several days of rest. The useful window depends on roast, packaging and brewing method.

A bag without a roast date can still contain acceptable coffee, especially when packed under protective conditions, but you have less information about its current state.

## Country, region and producer

Origin information may move from broad to specific:

> Colombia → Huila → Pitalito → producer or co-operative → individual lot

Country suggests broad context. Region narrows climate and local practices. Producer or washing-station information improves traceability and can help you follow future harvests.

Not every supply chain can print an individual farm. Smallholders may combine cherries through a co-operative or washing station. That is not automatically less transparent; the bag should describe the level that can be supported accurately.

## Species and variety

The bag may say Arabica, Robusta or Canephora. Speciality Arabica bags often list a specific variety such as Bourbon, Caturra, Typica, SL28, Pacamara or Gesha.

Variety can suggest flavour potential and agricultural traits, but it is not a guarantee. A famous variety grown or processed badly is still badly produced coffee.

If the bag says only "100% Arabica", you know the species but little about the particular coffee.

## Processing method

Common terms include:

- washed;
- natural;
- honey;
- wet-hulled;
- anaerobic or another fermentation description.

Processing helps set flavour expectations. Washed coffees often emphasise clarity, naturals often emphasise fruit and body, and honey processes may combine sweetness with moderate clarity.

Experimental terms need context. "Anaerobic" usually describes a fermentation environment, not the entire journey from cherry to dry seed.

## Altitude

Altitude may appear as metres above sea level, often written `masl`.

Higher elevation frequently brings cooler growing conditions and slower ripening, which can support dense, complex coffee. But latitude, climate, variety and farm management change what a number means.

Do not choose between 1,650 and 1,750 metres as if buying a taller coffee. Use altitude as one piece of origin context.

## Roast level and brew method

A bag may say light, medium or dark, or recommend filter, espresso or omni brewing.

Roast labels vary between roasters. The flavour description often tells you more:

- floral, citrus and tea-like notes usually suggest a lighter, origin-led profile;
- caramel, nut and chocolate notes often suggest medium development;
- smoke, dark chocolate and low acidity suggest a darker profile.

The recommendation is a starting point. Espresso-labelled coffee can work in a moka pot or AeroPress, and filter coffee can work as espresso if your equipment and patience agree.

## Flavour notes

Flavour notes describe sensory associations, not added ingredients.

"Blueberry, jasmine and chocolate" means the roaster found aspects of the coffee that reminded tasters of those things. It does not mean the beans were flavoured with them.

Use notes as a map of style:

- fruit and floral notes suggest brightness and aromatic complexity;
- chocolate, nuts and caramel suggest a familiar sweet profile;
- fermented, winey or tropical notes suggest a more processing-led character;
- smoky or roasty notes suggest stronger roast character.

You may not taste the exact words. Water, brewing and personal perception all change the cup.

## Harvest and crop information

Some bags include harvest year, season or crop. This is useful because green coffee also changes during storage. Harvest calendars differ by country, and well-stored green coffee can remain excellent beyond a simple "current crop" label.

Use harvest information as transparency, not an automatic rejection rule.

## Certifications and sourcing claims

Organic, Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance and other certifications refer to defined environmental, farming or trading requirements. They do not function as flavour scores.

Terms such as direct trade or relationship coffee may describe a roaster's sourcing approach, but they are not universally regulated. Look for supporting detail: producer names, length of relationship, price transparency or project information.

## Best-before date

Best-before dates support stock control and food labelling. They are often many months after roasting and do not describe peak aroma.

Use the roast date for freshness decisions and the best-before date as the manufacturer's unopened-quality limit. They answer different questions.

## A quick buying checklist

Ask:

1. Are the flavour notes in a family I usually enjoy?
2. Is the roast suitable for my brewing method and taste?
3. Does the process sound familiar or deliberately adventurous?
4. Is there a roast date or credible freshness information?
5. Is there enough origin detail to learn from the purchase?

The prettiest bag can contain excellent coffee. It can also contain a very expensive lesson in graphic design.

<div class="callout callout--exercise">

## Try this today

Take one coffee bag and write down every factual detail separately from every marketing phrase. Use the facts to predict flavour, then brew the coffee and compare your prediction with the cup.

<div class="callout callout--takeaway">

## Key takeaway

Read a coffee bag as a set of clues: origin, variety, process, roast, flavour notes and dates. No single field guarantees quality, but together they help you choose deliberately and repeat coffees you enjoy.
